About

I'm a PhD student in the Electrical and Computer Engineering (ECE) department at Purdue University. My work covers satellite image change detection, multivariate time-series forecasting on sensor data, and model compression for edge AI.

AI is everywhere now, but most models are still too heavy for real-world use. Foundation models eat memory and power, making them hard to run on constrained hardware. I think efficiency is a core part of good engineering, not an afterthought. That's the motivation behind my interest in model compression and efficient learning.

There's also a deeper issue: we don't really understand how models reason about time. Methods for temporal modeling exist, but truly capturing dynamic scenes and evolving content is still an open problem. That's the other thread running through my research.

Overall, I try to build efficient and interpretable models that actually hold up in the real world.

Problems I’m Interested In

Most of my research comes from a single observation: learning-based systems often fail in the wild, not because they lack capability, but because they weren’t built with real constraints in mind.

The most immediate constraint is computation. Modern models are overparameterized and expensive. Putting them on edge devices is genuinely hard. So I’m interested in figuring out what’s actually necessary inside a model and what can be cut without hurting performance. This isn’t just about speed; it’s about understanding what structure a model really needs.

Closely tied to this is the problem of temporal reasoning. Most systems I work with, satellite imagery, sensor networks, evolve over time. Static models don’t handle that well. Even models that claim to do temporal modeling often just pick up short-range correlations. I want to understand how information actually propagates over time and how to build representations that reflect real causal structure.

Change understanding fits naturally into this. In visual data, detecting that something changed is the easy part. Knowing what changed, where, and why it matters is much harder. A lot of apparent changes are just noise: lighting shifts, seasonal variation, sensor differences. Getting a model to focus on changes that are semantically meaningful is a problem I work on directly.

Finally, I think classical signal processing is underused. Decomposition methods and inverse problem formulations bring interpretability and theoretical grounding that pure deep learning lacks. Combining these with learned representations often gives you something more robust and more controllable. That combination is something I find genuinely useful, not just interesting.
