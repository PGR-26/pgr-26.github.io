About

I'm a PhD student in ECE at Purdue. My work covers satellite image change detection, multivariate time-series forecasting on sensor data, and model compression for edge deployment.

Foundation models have become the default for most engineering tasks in computer vision and time series modeling. But their widespread adoption has created new problems that go beyond a benchmark performance metric. While most academics focus on beating benchmarks in these fields, I believe that these new problems are more important than the bencmark metric. The first is efficiency. Large VLMs and time series networks require substantial compute even for inference. Deploying them on resource-constrained hardware demands more than compression heuristics. It requires understanding which representations and computations are structurally necessary, and which are redundant artifacts of how we train.

The second problem is semantic grounding. VLMs acquire associations from image-text co-occurrence, but co-occurrence is not understanding. Human perception is grounded in a model of the physical world: 3D geometry, causality, material properties etc. Models trained on statistical associations do NOT have these structural priors, which is why they fail at many tasks requiring even when surface-level semantics appear correct.

The third is temporality. Most vision models treat each image in isolation. Real scenes evolve, and understanding that evolution requires reasoning about how and why things change, not just detecting that a difference exists from scene to scene. This extends to sensor-based systems where temporal dependencies carry information about the underlying dynamics that no single frame can reveal.

These three problems form the core of what I work on.

Problems I’m Interested In

My interests converge on problems where standard deep learning approaches fail for principled reasons, not just empirical ones.

Efficient learning and model compression. The trend is to give high capacity to the models and regularize. But this tells us little about what information a model actually needs. I am interested in the problem of minimal sufficient representations: what is the minimum structural capacity(say number of parameters) required to solve a task, and what inductive biases can replace learned parameters without sacrificing performance? Compression, from this view, is not just optimization.

Temporal and spatial reasoning with semantics. Most sequence models are trained to minimize prediction error over short horizons, which rewards capturing statistics rather than the REAL underlying dynamical system. Genuine temporal reasoning requires models that represent state transitions, causal dependencies, and long-range influence. In visual data, this extends to scene dynamics: not just detecting change, but attributing it to a cause.

Graph-based learning. Many real-world signals are defined over relational structures that recent architectures ignore. Multivariate sensor networks and spatial image graphs are natural candidates for GNNs, which encode relational inductive biases directly into the model rather than expecting the architecture to discover them from data.

Diffusion models and structured inference. Beyond synthesis, diffusion models provide a framework for posterior inference through denoising. Their connection to score-based methods makes them applicable to inverse problems where the forward model is known, offering a way to incorporate domain constraints that purely discriminative approaches cannot.
